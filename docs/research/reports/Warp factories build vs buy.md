# Rent the factory floor, build the brain

**Recommendation: go hybrid.** Rent the execution layer and build the layers that make up the factory's "brain." Warp Factories is a strong but early product. It runs configuration-as-code pipelines of cloud agents that move a single work item through triage, spec, implementation and review, then hand off a PR. It also has the best built-in measurement and self-improvement loop of any vendor surveyed ([Warp Docs](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/)). Three of your requirements fall outside what Warp, or any other vendor, ships today: **agents that group and rank the whole backlog and decide what to work on**, **a team-owned portal with real isolation between teams like design and engineering**, and **self-improvement measured against business outcomes rather than LLM-judge rubrics**. Warp documents no backlog ranking. Per-team roles and workspaces are Enterprise-only. Its self-improvement loop only optimizes against scorer criteria you write ([How factories work](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/how-factories-work/), [Permissions](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/permissions/)). Warp is also still **Early Access, with a `v1alpha1` config schema and a proprietary orchestration server** ([Factory as code](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/factory-as-code/), [The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/warp-software-factory-infrastructure/)). Committing your whole system to it is therefore a lock-in risk. Building everything is also a poor choice. The firms that have done it (Stripe, Spotify, Uber) relied on years of existing devbox, CI and gateway infrastructure. The practical path is to pilot Warp, or GitHub Agentic Workflows, as a replaceable "factory floor." You build three pieces yourself: an intake-and-ranking service, a portal layer, and an outcome-metrics warehouse, all behind a thin interface you control. If the rented layer fails you, you can then swap in Temporal plus an open-source harness without losing the parts that set your system apart.

## Warp Factories is a single-item agent pipeline, delivered as configuration

A Warp **factory** is a versioned tree of YAML and Markdown rooted at `factory.yaml`. It covers one set of repositories and declares their agents, automations, runners, model routers, skills, webhooks, benchmarks and scorers ([Factory as code](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/factory-as-code/)). Each request becomes a **work item** that keeps its identity from intake to handoff. A coordinating **foreman** agent, addressable as `@<factory-name>` in Slack or Linear, routes the item through the stages **Triage → Planning → Building → Reviewing → Human handoff → Complete**. A revision loop runs from review back to build, and the foreman "picks the shortest path that still meets your quality policy" ([How factories work](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/how-factories-work/)). Work can enter from Slack, Teams, GitHub, GitLab, Azure DevOps, Linear, Jira, cron schedules, signed webhooks, the REST API and SDKs, or a local coding agent through the Factory MCP. CI signals arrive as GitHub events such as `check_suite_completed` filtered to failures; Warp is not itself a CI runner ([Automation triggers](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/automation-triggers/)). Runs execute in Warp-hosted sandboxes by default. Warp supports multiple models and harnesses, including Claude Code and Codex, and Enterprise customers can self-host execution ([Warp launch blog](https://www.warp.dev/blog/open-infrastructure-for-building-a-software-factory)).

Two design choices shape how well Warp fits your goals. **"The factory never merges on your behalf"**: merges stay under branch protection, and the documented lifecycle ends at the merge, with no deploy or monitor stage ([How factories work](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/how-factories-work/)). Warp also tells you to group repos that ship together into one factory and **not to split a repo set by team**. Its recommended way to specialize within a factory is custom agents and skills ([Warp Docs](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/)). A design team gets its own factory only if it owns its own repos.

Measurement is where Warp stands out. Each factory's dashboard reports **Autonomy** (the share of merged PRs with no human code push), **PR cycle time** broken out by stage, and **cost per PR** split into compute, platform and inference ([Factory dashboard](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/factory-dashboard/)). On top of that, LLM-judge **Scorers** classify completed runs against criteria you write. **Benchmarks** compare model and runner configurations on fixed tasks. **Self-improvement** triggers when an agent has accumulated 25 unreviewed scorer failures, or when the oldest is 7 days old. It groups the failures and opens PRs against code, prompts, skills or config, and nothing is adopted without review ([Self-improvement](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/measure-and-improve/self-improvement/)).

The product's maturity is thin. Warp launched Factories as a closed beta on **August 18, 2026**, with $10k of free usage for qualified orgs ([Fortune press release](https://fortune.com/press-releases/warp-launches-warp-factories-automate-software-development-2026-08-18/)). In November 2026 the docs still label it Early Access. Warp's own claims of value are modest: the company automates **about 30–35% of its own tasks weekly**, and CEO Zach Lloyd says the main target is smaller companies that can't build a factory themselves ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/18/warps-new-system-is-an-out-of-the-box-software-factory-for-ai-development/)). The launch post names no customers ([Warp launch blog](https://www.warp.dev/blog/open-infrastructure-for-building-a-software-factory)), and the research found no substantive independent user reviews on HN or Reddit. Pricing combines a platform tier with metered usage:

| Tier | Price | What matters for you |
|---|---|---|
| Pay as you go | $0 + usage at 20% above API rates | Built-in and custom agents, Warp-hosted config |
| Build | $20/mo + usage at API rates | Claude Code and Codex harnesses, **GitHub-backed factory-as-code** |
| Business | $50/user/mo | SAML SSO, BYO API keys and custom inference endpoints |
| Enterprise | Custom | **Workspaces across teams**, role assignment, self-hosted workers, BYO AI provider |

Source: [Warp pricing](https://www.warp.dev/pricing), [Permissions](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/permissions/).

## Warp covers four of your seven requirements, and none of the three that set your system apart

The table below scores Warp against each requirement you stated. "Gap" means you would build the capability yourself, either on Warp's primitives or outside Warp.

| Requirement | Warp coverage | Evidence and gap |
|---|---|---|
| Submit features and issues | **Strong** | Linear, Jira, GitHub, GitLab, ADO, Slack, Teams, API and webhook intake. Results post back to the source thread ([Warp Docs](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/)). |
| CI/CD processes such as stress tests feeding the pipeline | **Partial** | Cron, `workflow_run_completed` and `check_suite_completed` triggers can open work items when a load test fails ([Automation triggers](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/automation-triggers/)). The test itself still runs in your CI, and Warp offers no process-template registry. |
| Agents group and rank tickets and decide what to work on | **Gap** | Triage handles one issue at a time against a categories-and-priorities skill. The docs describe no backlog-wide grouping, no ranking, and no rule for how priority affects ordering. Work is selected only by intake labels, filters and schedules ([Issue triage](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/use-cases/issue-triage/), [End-to-end SDLC](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/use-cases/end-to-end-sdlc-automation/)). |
| Automated implement, test and review | **Strong** | Spec, implement (PR with test and visual evidence) and review agents, plus computer-use verification on Linux and Mac ([How factories work](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/how-factories-work/)). Merge stays human, and deploy and monitor are not covered. |
| Central portal where each team sets up its own factory | **Partial, Enterprise-gated** | The "Factory Home" web app lists every factory, with a "Waiting on you" queue. **Assigning user and editor roles requires Enterprise; on other plans every member is an Editor.** Workspaces across teams are Enterprise-only, and there is no template gallery ([Permissions](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/permissions/), [Factory dashboard](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/factory-dashboard/)). |
| System measures itself | **Strong per factory, weak org-wide** | Autonomy, cycle time and cost per PR for each factory. The docs describe no roll-up across factories and no metrics export or OpenTelemetry ([Factory dashboard](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/factory-dashboard/)). |
| System improves itself | **Partial** | The Scorer → Self-improvement PR loop is real, but you can't target specific agents or failures, and it optimizes only against scorer rubrics, not merge survival, reverts or business value ([Self-improvement](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/measure-and-improve/self-improvement/)). |

The gaps cluster in one place. Warp does well at the *execution* of individual work items. It is thin on *portfolio* decisions (what to build next, and across which teams) and on *outcome* feedback (did the merged code survive and help). Those are the parts of your system that make it distinctive. Every gap can technically be built on Warp's primitives. For example, a `schedule`-triggered "planner" agent could query Linear over MCP, cluster and rank issues, and apply a `factory-ready` label that starts the standard pipeline. But you would own that logic either way. It should therefore live in a component you control, not inside a `v1alpha1` config on a proprietary server.

## Rival vendors split the same capabilities differently, and none ranks a backlog

The market moved quickly in 2026. Almost every major vendor can now launch a cloud agent from a tracker, Slack, a schedule or a webhook. Where they differ is breadth of triggers, governance and self-measurement. **No commercial product found ranks a whole backlog and decides what to work on.**

| Product | Strongest fit for you | Key weakness for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Warp Factories** | Configuration-as-code pipeline, autonomy and cost metrics, built-in self-improvement PR loop | Early Access; per-team RBAC is Enterprise-only; no ranking; proprietary server |
| **Devin Automations** | Broadest triggers (CI check runs, GitLab pipelines, PagerDuty, Linear "priority changed"), a **Triage Devin** monitor that spawns child sessions, and preflight scripts that can fan one event into up to 50 work items ([Devin Docs](https://docs.devin.ai/product-guides/automations)) | No analytics dashboard confirmed; full queues drop events; conflicting pricing reports |
| **GitHub Agentic Workflows** | **GA since Oct 28, 2026**. Markdown workflows compile to Actions and reuse runner groups, environment gates and org budgets. Engines include Claude Code and Codex ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/11/github-agentic-workflows-ga/)). Its off-the-shelf issue-triage agent sets priority labels and flags duplicates ([gh-aw gallery](https://github.github.com/gh-aw/gallery/ai-issue-triage/)) | GitHub-only; little observability; practitioners caution against using it for code-changing workflows |
| **Linear Triage Intelligence** | Only first-party **duplicate and related-issue detection** with per-property auto-apply, and triage rules can delegate to agents on Business and Enterprise ([Linear docs](https://linear.app/docs/triage-intelligence)) | Does not suggest priority; still beta; one user reported about 85% duplicate accuracy ([workmanagementhub](https://workmanagementhub.com/linear-agent-feature-deep-dive-2026/)) |
| **Factory.ai Droids** | "Agent Effectiveness" analytics with friction signals (looping, undone work) and attribution of spend to intent ([Factory](https://factory.com/news/agent-effectiveness)) | Hard-to-forecast cost, opaque capacity limits ([Ry Walker](https://rywalker.com/research/factory-ai)) |
| **Cursor Cloud Agents** | Richest enterprise spend, access and audit controls ([Cursor pricing](https://cursor.com/pricing)) | Intake and triage depend on Linear or GitHub |
| **Claude Code scheduled tasks** | Cheap judgment work from a schedule, the `/fire` API or GitHub events, with self-hosted environments | **Tasks belong to individual accounts and are not shared with teammates** ([Claude Code Docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/routines)), which disqualifies them as a team portal |
| **Port** (developer portal) | Exposes every self-service workflow as an MCP tool and documents a "triage tickets, pass only well-defined ones to coding agents" pattern ([Port docs](https://docs.port.io/guides/all/triage-tickets-to-coding-agents)) | Commercial SaaS; it is a portal, not an execution engine |

Two conclusions follow. For a GitHub-centric organization, **GitHub Agentic Workflows is the strongest rival to Warp as the execution layer**. It is generally available, inherits mature Actions governance, and stores each team's workflow as Markdown in that team's repo. Warp wins on built-in measurement and self-improvement, and on supporting multiple forges and trackers. Second, the "buy" answer to grouping and ranking is assembled, not purchased: Linear handles deduplication and routing, an agent vendor handles execution, and a custom component handles ranking. That makes ranking the first thing you must build no matter which platform you choose. The market is also unstable. **Terragon and Bloop shut down and Sweep is unmaintained** ([software-factories list](https://github.com/pranshuchittora/agent-qa-software-factories-20261031)), which argues for keeping any vendor behind a swap layer.

## Build the brain: intake and ranking, the portal, and outcome metrics

The documented in-house factories share one lesson: **the agent is the smallest part.** Stripe's Minions produce **more than 1,000 merged PRs per week**. They run on a fork of the open-source Goose harness, in pre-warmed devboxes that start in about 10 seconds, with an internal MCP server exposing more than 400 tools, and they allow at most two CI rounds ([Stripe Dev Blog](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents)). Spotify's Honk puts a swappable-agent CLI behind Backstage, and Spotify says that design let it replace components several times ([Spotify Engineering](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/spotifys-background-coding-agent-part-1)). Port's synthesis of 13 companies names nine recurring patterns: an MCP gateway, spend control, a background agent, a model router, shared context, quality gates, ephemeral environments, an identity layer and guardrails ([Port](https://software-factories.port.io/)). The architecture below builds only what Warp and its rivals lack, and leaves room to swap the execution layer later.

| Layer | Build or buy | Recommended component |
|---|---|---|
| **Portal and factory registry** | Build on open source, or buy Port | Backstage catalog: each factory is a catalog entity owned by a team, each pipeline template is a scaffolder Template, and the MCP Actions backend lets agents read and run templates ([Backstage AI docs](https://backstage.io/docs/next/ai/)) |
| **Intake, dedup, grouping, ranking** | **Build** | Postgres with pgvector, an LLM normalizer, a pairwise LLM judge, and deterministic scoring code |
| **Process templates (stress and load tests)** | Build thin | k6 or Locust scripts with thresholds, run deterministically by your CI or k6-operator; agents consume results, trigger runs, or author scripts as PRs |
| **Execution (spec, implement, review)** | **Buy now**, keep swappable | Warp Factories or GitHub Agentic Workflows behind an internal `factory.submit()` API; fallback is Temporal plus Goose, the OpenHands SDK or the Claude Agent SDK in Kubernetes Jobs ([Temporal](https://temporal.io/blog/announcing-openai-agents-sdk-integration)) |
| **Outcome metrics and evals** | **Build** | OpenTelemetry GenAI spans into self-hosted Langfuse or Phoenix, joined to git, CI and incident data by PR ID |
| **Self-improvement** | Build on vendor loop | Mine review comments, CI failures and reverts into candidate rules, validate them against a private replay suite, and open a PR for human approval |

The **ranking service** is the piece that sets your system apart, and the evidence on how to build it is specific. Linear's approach, embedding retrieval of candidates followed by a slower LLM judgment that a human accepts, is the proven dedup pattern ([Linear blog](https://linear.app/blog/using-ai-to-detect-similar-issues)). The pipeline should first extract structured fields such as component, version and repro steps. It then retrieves the top-k candidates with a **pinned** embedding model, because cosine thresholds break when the model changes. Next it asks an LLM whether each pair is a duplicate, related, or distinct. Finally it forms groups from connected components over duplicate edges only, since embeddings measure relatedness, not identity, and raw clustering falsely merges issues ([DZone](https://dzone.com/articles/semantic-triage-engine-at-scale), [technetexperts](https://www.technetexperts.com/fix-llm-embedding-similarity-overlap/)). Ranking deserves more skepticism. The research found **no study validating LLM-computed RICE or WSJF scores**. Grafana's SLO team dropped RICE as inaccurate in favor of a simpler 1–5 scheme ([Grafana](https://grafana.com/blog/a-better-way-to-prioritize-feature-backlogs-the-cerb-scoring-method)), and one vendor measured raw frontier models drifting **7.5–9.8% run to run** on theme counts ([Enterpret](https://www.enterpret.com/guides/the-5-ways-ai-native-product-teams-prioritize-instead-of-rice-ice-and-kano)). So the LLM should estimate the inputs (reach, effort, confidence) with a written rationale, and deterministic code should compute the score, with humans able to override it. "Decide what to work on" should be driven by an **agent-readiness gate**, not by priority alone: only well-specified, small-scope tickets go to agents, and the rest go to a clarification step or to humans. Port, Spotify and Stripe all use this pattern ([Port docs](https://docs.port.io/guides/all/triage-tickets-to-coding-agents)). The service's output is simply a label or API call that starts the rented pipeline.

The **portal** maps cleanly onto Backstage. A team registers a factory entity that names its repos, harness, review policy, CI gates and optional load-test profile. A scaffolder action such as `factory:start` then provisions the matching Warp factory, or gh-aw workflow, from a team template. This gives you team ownership and a template gallery without paying for Warp Enterprise just to get roles. Design teams are the open question. **No case study was found of a design team running its own agent factory**, and no vendor offers Figma-triggered agents. A design factory is therefore an experiment. It would most likely start from visual-regression tooling, using screenshot baselines for Storybook or Playwright that agents review over MCP ([software-factories list](https://github.com/pranshuchittora/agent-qa-software-factories-20261031)).

The **metrics warehouse** fixes the weakest part of every vendor's self-measurement. Published merge rates for agent PRs vary by about 40 percentage points depending on the denominator, and a high merge rate can simply reflect self-merging ([Amplifying.ai](https://amplifying.ai/coding-agents/autonomy.md)). Your scorecard should track merge rate, separated by human-reviewed versus self-merged. It should also track cost per merged PR, human takeover rate, 30- and 90-day line survival, revert rate, review-comment addressed rate, and DORA change-failure rate. Change-failure rate is the counterweight because DORA 2025 found that AI raises throughput while encouraging larger changesets that strain stability ([Google DORA](https://blog.google/technology/developers/dora-report-2025/)). Survival is measurable: agent-written lines were modified less often than human lines in a study of 201 repos, though the result varied widely by agent ([arXiv 2601.16809](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16809)). Because OpenTelemetry's GenAI conventions are still in "Development" status and the observability vendors are consolidating (ClickHouse bought Langfuse, Dynatrace bought Arize), instrument with OTel spans and keep the backend swappable ([Greptime](https://www.greptime.com/blogs/2026-05-09-opentelemetry-genai-semantic-conventions)).

For **self-improvement**, the strongest production evidence favors mining review feedback into rules with a human approval step. Cursor's Bugbot generated more than 44,000 learned rules across more than 110,000 repos, activating rules on positive signal and pruning them on negative signal. Its resolution rate rose from 52% to 78%, though Cursor does not isolate how much of that came from learning ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/bugbot-learning)). Pydantic distilled 10,020 review comments into AGENTS.md files for about **$63** ([Pydantic](https://pydantic.dev/articles/scaling-open-source-with-ai)). Reflective prompt optimization such as GEPA suits narrow components with cheap metrics, and your ranking prompt is the obvious candidate ([arXiv 2507.19457](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.19457)). Self-modifying harnesses remain research projects at roughly $22k per run ([GonzoML](https://gonzoml.substack.com/p/darwin-godel-machine)). Every loop needs guardrails against reward hacking. In ImpossibleBench, agents rewrote tests that contradicted the spec. Making tests read-only blocked those edits without hurting legitimate success, and an explicit "STOP if tests are flawed" instruction cut GPT-5's hacking rate on the conflicting LiveCodeBench variant from about 92% to 1% ([ImpossibleBench](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/qJYMbrabcQqCZ7iqm/impossiblebench-measuring-reward-hacking-in-llm-coding-1)). Agent PRs should therefore never be able to weaken protected test paths without human sign-off.

## A four-phase plan that keeps the vendor decision reversible

The phases are ordered so that each builds a capability you keep, regardless of which execution vendor survives. The research found no published headcount or cost for in-house factories, so the effort figures are inferences. Treat them as planning ranges, not benchmarks. The build notes estimate 2–4 engineers for a quarter for a single-template MVP, and a 4–8 person platform team over 6–12 months for multi-team production.

| Phase | Timeline | Scope | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Baseline and pilot** | Weeks 0–6 | Capture pre-factory baselines: cycle time, change-failure rate, review load. Request Warp access and run one repo set on the default pipeline with a GitHub-backed config (Build tier). In parallel, try gh-aw issue triage on one repo. Wrap both behind a minimal `factory.submit()` API. | Autonomy, cost per PR and survival measured on 50 or more items, with a written comparison of Warp and gh-aw |
| **1. Intake and ranking service** | Months 2–4 | Postgres with pgvector, normalize → retrieve → judge → group, deterministic scoring, an agent-readiness gate, and a human review queue. A cron job hands the top N items to the execution layer through a label. Register stress tests as cron or CI-failure triggers. | Dedup precision checked by hand on a sample; ranking accepted by humans at least 70% of the time (pick your own threshold) |
| **2. Portal and multi-team** | Months 4–8 | Backstage factory entities and scaffolder templates per team (engineering first, design as an experiment), a process-template registry for k6 or Locust, per-team budgets, scoped GitHub App identities, and an OTel metrics warehouse with a cross-team scorecard | Two or more teams self-serve a factory without platform-team help |
| **3. Self-improvement and vendor decision** | Months 8–12 | A 20–50 task private replay suite drawn from your own failures ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)), nightly rule mining into PRs, GEPA tuning of the ranking prompt, and protected-test CI checks. Make the formal decision whether Warp (now hopefully GA) stays, or whether execution moves to Temporal plus an open-source harness. | Measured improvement against the Phase 0 baseline on outcome metrics, not on sentiment |

Three caveats determine how fast to move. First, Warp's Enterprise tier is required for per-team roles. If engineering and design need hard isolation inside Warp itself, rather than through your portal, get an Enterprise quote before Phase 2. Second, rigorous productivity evidence is still weak. METR's 2026 follow-up found speedups that were not statistically significant, and its 2025 study found developers believed they were faster while actually being 19% slower ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). Every phase gate should therefore rest on measured outcomes. Third, review capacity is likely to become the bottleneck. Stripe and Spotify started with migrations, flaky tests and well-scoped tasks, and you should too.

## Conclusion

Asking "build or buy" frames the decision too broadly. The real line falls between *execution*, which commoditized during 2026 as Warp, GitHub, Devin, Cursor and Anthropic converged on the same cloud-agent primitives, and *judgment*: deciding what is worth building, for which team, and whether the result held up after merge. No vendor sells judgment, and it is also where your organization's own data (backlog history, review comments, incidents) gives you an advantage no vendor can copy. Rent the commodity, own the judgment, and keep the boundary between them a thin API you control.

Warp's most useful contribution may be its design rather than its runtime. Its definitions-as-code layout, autonomy and cost-per-PR metrics, and scorer-driven improvement PRs are a reasonable blueprint even if you later move execution elsewhere. The open risk is that a GA release of Warp, or a competitor, adds backlog ranking and cross-team workspaces and absorbs what you built in Phases 1 and 2. That outcome is acceptable: a ranking service that already feeds a pluggable execution layer turns a vendor's new feature into an easy migration rather than a loss.
